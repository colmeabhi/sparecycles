\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

SpareCycles shows that a small team can build a volunteer computing platform that tolerates the three faults of Section~\ref{sec:goals} with algorithms it writes itself. Leases and SWIM gossip move the work of crashed volunteers within seconds, majority voting and trust-based resource discovery keep faulty volunteers from changing results, and Bully election with terms, together with primary-backup replication of the task table, removes the coordinator as a single point of failure. Each failure is reproducible with one command, and the results in this report come from recorded runs and automated tests.

We draw three lessons. First, building the complete system with trivial stubs before any real algorithm, and then replacing one stub at a time behind a stable interface, let the three of us work in parallel and kept the system runnable throughout; the end-to-end failure scripts were as valuable as the unit tests, because they exposed interactions, such as late copies escaping the vote, that no single package showed. Second, fault tolerance has a measurable price. Redundant computation costs about a third of the throughput with four volunteers, and every task-table change waits for a backup. Third, the gap between a textbook algorithm and a safe one can be large. Bully is correct only under synchrony; making it safe under partitions took term numbers, majority acceptance, a leader lease and a catch-up step, which is much of the machinery of a consensus protocol.

Several improvements would make the system faster and more robust. \emph{Adaptive replication}, as in BOINC, could use our trust scores to run units from well-established volunteers with fewer copies, and \emph{cancelling the third copy} once two copies agree would remove most of the wasted executions. Catch-up should wait for a majority of snapshots before a new leader acts, and trust could be kept per token and replicated, so that it survives a failover and a re-registration. \emph{TLS} on all connections would stop an eavesdropper from copying session secrets. \emph{Availability forecasting} as in VECA~\cite{yeddulapalli2024veca} could steer units toward volunteers that are likely to stay online, and \emph{confidential computing} with hardware enclaves would allow workloads with private data. Finally, running the Terraform deployment across three availability zones would test failover and gossip over a real wide-area network.
